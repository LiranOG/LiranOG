## What I am trying to say, in one sentence

Most people are operating on autopilot in a world that no longer permits autopilot, and we are running out of time to wake up.

## On autopilot, and why it used to be fine

For roughly three hundred thousand years, autopilot was a survival strategy. Most of what a human being needed to do to stay alive was already encoded in instincts shaped by evolutionary pressures we did not have to consciously navigate. Find food. Avoid threats. Stay close to the tribe. Make alliances. Detect cheaters. Notice when someone is angry. Remember who helped you. Pursue status within a group of fifty to a hundred and fifty people. Defend the group from outsiders. Have children. Protect them. Die.

This was not a small accomplishment. The instincts that let us do this work are extraordinarily sophisticated. They run beneath conscious thought, they update faster than deliberate reasoning, and they have kept the human line alive across ice ages, plagues, famines, and migrations that wiped out most of the other hominid species that walked alongside us.

The instincts are also calibrated for a world that no longer exists.

The world they were calibrated for had: small groups, limited resources, slow-changing environments, no global communication, no global supply chains, no nuclear weapons, no biotech that could engineer pathogens in a graduate student's basement, no economies that could collapse continents in twenty-four hours, no algorithms shaping the attention of billions of people simultaneously, no asteroids we could in principle deflect if we coordinated, and no questions about whether the technology we are now creating will turn out to be aligned with the species that built it.

The instincts are still there. The world they were built for is gone.

This is not a sentimental observation. It is a structural one. The thing that used to be a survival strategy — running on autopilot, deferring to tribal allegiances, chasing status within a small group, treating the environment as unchanging and the future as something that arrives slowly — is now a failure mode. And most people, most of the time, are still running on it.

You can see the old software surface under pressure. Put an ordinary person under acute stress — financial collapse, substance use, threat to their social position — and the masks come off. The politeness, the self-regulation, the considered responses — these are a thin layer over instincts that were shaped for small-group survival and have never been fully overwritten. Under pressure, the primate resurfaces: aggression, tribalism, zero-sum thinking, fight-or-flight responses applied to situations that require the exact opposite. This is not a moral judgment. It is an observation about how thin the veneer of modern behavior is, and about how reliably it cracks when the conditions change.

I do not blame them. I do not exempt myself from this. The autopilot is not a moral failing; it is the default state of the species. But I think it is now actively dangerous, and I think the cost of recognizing this is so much smaller than the cost of not recognizing it that I cannot stay quiet about it.

And it is getting worse. The autopilot used to be just instinct — pattern-matching shaped by evolution, running beneath conscious awareness. Now there is a second layer on top of it: an algorithmic environment that has learned to predict and exploit the instinct faster than the person can notice it is happening. The platform knows what you will click before you have decided whether you actually want to. The feed knows which emotion will keep you scrolling before you have had time to ask whether scrolling serves you. The autopilot is no longer just biological. It is biological *plus* an infrastructure that has been optimized, at planetary scale, to keep it running.

The mismatch in speed makes this structural, not accidental. A platform's algorithm makes content decisions in milliseconds. A person needs at least half a second to override a habit and three to five seconds to actually deliberate. A court of law takes months to reach a ruling. A legislature takes years to pass meaningful regulation. The fastest subsystems in the civilization — the ones that shape what billions of people see and feel every day — are running orders of magnitude faster than the subsystems designed to govern them. By the time the slow systems respond, the fast ones have already moved.

## On chasing money and status, and the lie that the ego tells

A specific version of autopilot deserves its own paragraph because it is the version most people actually live inside without examining.

Most modern lives are organized around the pursuit of money and status. We tell ourselves a great many stories about why this is true — that money is freedom, that status is recognition, that achievement is meaningful, that we are providing for our families, that we are leaving a mark, that we are winning at the game we were taught to play. Some of these stories are partially true. None of them are the whole truth.

The whole truth, as far as I can see it, is that the pursuit of money and status is a tribal instinct running on hardware that no longer has a tribe. The status games we play are not games against the universe or against our actual situation in it. They are games against neighbors, peers, colleagues, strangers on the internet who do not know we exist. The money we accumulate beyond what we need to live is a number that represents nothing real about us. The followers, the likes, the titles, the offices, the things — these are coordinates in a tribal hierarchy that the tribe has, in any meaningful sense, dissolved.

I am not saying money and status do not matter. They obviously matter. The system we live inside makes them matter. You need money to eat, to have shelter, to take care of the people you love, to have the time and tools to do work that matters. Status — within reason — gets you access to people and resources and conversations that you cannot reach without it.

But the status game is more subtle than it looks. Within any small group — a family, a friend circle, a work team — there are unwritten rules that nobody wrote down but everybody enforces. What is funny. What is off-limits. Who speaks first. Who gets interrupted. Whose ideas get adopted and whose get politely ignored. There is a private language of inside jokes and shorthand and shared references. There is a hidden hierarchy that everyone can feel but no one acknowledges openly. And there is an implicit audition happening at all times: the ego is scanning for signals about where it stands, adjusting its behavior to be accepted, wearing different masks for different groups. The small group is a laboratory. In it, we test who we are allowed to be, and the tribe hands us an immediate, merciless grade.

What is interesting is that people do not operate on a single social logic. In the same day, the same person can share resources freely with their family at dinner (communal sharing), accept a hierarchy at work without question (authority ranking), split a bill evenly with friends (equality matching), and negotiate a salary with a stranger (market pricing). These are four distinct modes of relating to other people, and we switch between them constantly, often without noticing, depending on the situation. The switch is not random — it responds to specific cues: resource scarcity pushes toward market logic; external threat pushes toward communal solidarity; task complexity pushes toward hierarchy. The point is that human social life is not one thing. It is at least four overlapping logics that the same person runs in parallel, and the ego is the system that manages the transitions.

What I am saying is this: when the pursuit of those things becomes the entire content of a life, the person doing the pursuing has been deceived. Not by anyone in particular. Deceived by their own ego, which is still running its tribal calibration software in a world that no longer has the tribe it was calibrated for.

The ego operates, at its deepest level, on a single axis: approach or avoid. Love or fear. Move toward what promises belonging, recognition, meaning — or move away from what threatens survival, status, exclusion. In a tribal world, both directions were constrained by reality. In the modern world, the fear side has been industrialized. Institutions have learned that fear produces faster, more predictable behavior than love — and so the systems we live inside disproportionately amplify the avoidance signal. National security, insurance, algorithmic anxiety, performance metrics — these are fear, processed at scale, translated into policy.

The ego will tell you that more is better, that the next promotion is what you needed, that the next purchase will complete you, that the next public acknowledgment will finally let you rest. The ego is wrong. It has always been wrong. But in a tribal context, being wrong in that direction was relatively safe — there were natural limits to how much you could acquire, and the tribe enforced its own constraints. In a global context with effectively unlimited room for accumulation and an algorithmic media environment that constantly tells you that you are not yet enough, the same ego that used to keep you alive in a tribe of a hundred will keep you running toward a horizon that recedes faster than you approach it, for as long as you live — and the risk is reaching the end without having asked the questions that would have given it meaning.

This is the lie. It is the worst lie a person can tell themselves. I suspect most of us tell it most of the time. I know I do.

I notice myself telling it. I have to actively work to interrupt it. The point is not that I have escaped it — the point is that the first step is noticing it is there at all.

## On the worst lie, which is the lie to yourself

The worst lie is not the one you tell to other people. It is the one you tell yourself.

There is a hierarchy of dishonesty, and I want to be specific about where its worst form sits.

The lies we tell to others — about what we know, about what we have done, about what we feel — are obviously bad. They damage trust, they corrode relationships, they make the social world less workable for everyone. There is a long tradition of writing about why honesty toward others matters, and I do not have anything new to add to it.

The lies we tell to ourselves are worse. They are worse because they cannot be corrected by anyone outside us. If I lie to you and you find out, you can confront me. The lie has a chance of being uncovered. If I lie to myself and I do not know I am doing it, no one outside me can fix the situation, because no one outside me knows what I really think.

And self-deception has a specific damage that other-deception does not: it closes the door to learning. You cannot update a belief you refuse to examine. You cannot revise a position you have convinced yourself is non-negotiable. You cannot grow in a direction you have made invisible to yourself. The lie to yourself is the lie that compounds, because every other piece of evidence about the world has to be twisted to be consistent with it, and the twisting gets more elaborate over time, until you are spending most of your mental energy maintaining a model of reality that has very little to do with reality.

The reason I keep coming back to this is that I think most of what is wrong with the world right now is downstream of it. Most of the people in positions to affect outcomes — in governments, in corporations, in scientific institutions, in media — are running on autopilot, and one of the most reliable features of autopilot is that it generates beautiful, internally consistent narratives about why the autopilot is, in fact, deliberate reasoning. People convince themselves they are thinking when they are pattern-matching. They convince themselves they are choosing when they are being choreographed.

This is not accidental. The categories through which we perceive the world — what counts as success, what counts as a threat, what counts as normal — are absorbed before we are capable of evaluating them. School, family, media, religion, platforms: they install the operating system before the user has the tools to inspect it. By the time you have the cognitive resources to question a worldview, the worldview is already running your perception. Critique comes after categorization, and the categories are already loaded.

And here is the strangest part: even the knowledge we produce *about* this system becomes part of it. When social science describes how people behave under certain conditions, and that description is taught in universities and absorbed into policy and fed into platform design, the description changes the thing it was describing. The system observes itself through us, and the observation alters what is being observed. This is not a metaphor. It is a measurable feedback loop — one that used to run slowly enough (over decades, through academic publishing and education) to allow correction. Now, with platforms feeding behavioral data back into their own design in real time, the self-observation loop has been compressed to the point where there is no time for reflection between observation and action.

I am not exempt from this. I run on autopilot more than I would like to admit. I tell myself stories. I have ego attachments to my own ideas — including the ideas in these documents. The aspiration is not to escape this entirely, which I do not think is possible. The aspiration is to keep noticing.

Truth is what I am after, not the comfort of being right. Truth is what makes the noticing worthwhile. Truth is what makes me sleep at night. Not truth as comforting belief — truth as the thing that is, regardless of whether I find it convenient. I would rather be wrong and know it than be wrong and be deceived about it. I would rather live in a world I can perceive accurately than in a world that flatters me. I would rather lose an argument honestly than win it by misrepresenting what the other person said.

This is not a unique virtue. It is the minimum entry requirement for being a serious person. I do not claim to clear it consistently. I claim that it is the standard I am trying to clear, and that publishing speculative theoretical work I know will be criticized is one way of testing whether I am willing to pay the cost of clearing it.

This is why I publish things before they are polished. Not because I am careless, but because the alternative — keeping ideas private until I am certain they are correct — is a form of self-deception. I will never be certain. The only way to get closer to correct is to put the work out and let people who know more than me find what is wrong with it.

I know some of what I have built is flawed. I know some of my theoretical claims will not survive contact with specialists in the relevant fields. I accept this. What I will not accept is the alternative: building in isolation until the work is safe, and then sharing something so hedged and softened that it cannot actually be wrong — and therefore cannot actually be right, either.

## On complexity, fractality, and the structure of what exists

I love complexity. Not complexity as an aesthetic — complexity as a fact of reality.

The observation that the universe is structured fractally — that the same logic of "things composed of smaller things, themselves composed of smaller things, all the way down, and the same logic running upward, all the way up" — repeats at every scale we have looked at — is, to me, one of the most genuinely strange features of reality. We do not have a good explanation for why it should be this way. It is just how things are organized.

Walk down the scales: galaxies cluster into superclusters. Superclusters contain galaxy clusters. Galaxy clusters contain galaxies. Galaxies contain star clusters and individual stars. Stars are at the center of planetary systems. Planets host ecosystems. Ecosystems are composed of organisms. Organisms are composed of cells. Cells are composed of organelles and molecules. Molecules are composed of atoms. Atoms are composed of nucleons and electrons. Nucleons are composed of quarks. Quarks may be composed of something we have not yet identified.

And in the other direction: a neuron fires. A pattern of neurons fires. A thought emerges. A behavior emerges. A social dynamic emerges. A cultural norm emerges. A civilization emerges. A civilization interacts with its environment — with the planet, with its climate, with the rest of the biosphere it shares the planet with.

None of these levels are separable. Not really. They influence each other in ways we are only beginning to map. The thing that keeps me at my desk — that keeps me building numerical relativity codes and writing speculative theoretical frameworks and analyzing SDSS data and modeling weird atmospheric phenomena — is that understanding this structure, even imperfectly, even partially, is one of the most important things a person can spend a life on. Not because it makes you look smart. Because the alternative is navigating a system you do not understand, in a world that no longer permits navigating systems you do not understand.

The dependencies between scales are not symbolic. They are concrete. If every mosquito on Earth disappeared tomorrow, the ecological consequences would cascade through fish populations, bird populations, plant pollination, soil chemistry, and human disease vectors in ways that would reshape the biosphere within a generation. If every bee disappeared, the consequences would be more drastic still. These are not abstractions. They are predictions about what would happen to the system if you removed one of its components.

The universe we live in is not a stack of independent systems that we can intervene in one at a time without consequence. It is a coupled fractal hierarchy in which every action propagates outward and inward through dependencies most of us do not perceive. The civilizations that survive their own technological transitions are going to be the civilizations that understand this. The ones that do not — that continue to treat the planet as an inexhaustible resource pool, the atmosphere as an infinite waste sink, and the future as somebody else's problem — are not going to make it.

This fractal logic does not stop at ecosystems. It runs through institutions. At the individual level, a person feels solidarity with their neighbors — and at the civilizational level, that feeling is translated into taxation and social insurance. At the individual level, a person feels fear — and at the civilizational level, that fear is translated into national security budgets, surveillance infrastructure, and military spending. Individual curiosity becomes research funding. Individual grief becomes memorial policy. Individual disgust at contamination becomes public health regulation. Individual guilt about inequality becomes charity, corporate social responsibility, and progressive taxation. Individual envy at unfair advantage becomes antitrust law. Individual anger becomes litigation instead of revenge. Individual desire becomes marriage contracts and family legislation.

The emotions are real; the institutions are the machinery that processes them at scale. And because the machinery operates above the scale any individual can perceive, almost no one notices that their private feelings have become public infrastructure.

The leap that makes all of this possible is one of the strangest things our species has done: at some point, as the group size exceeded what any individual could personally know, we stopped directing our deepest emotions at actual people and started directing them at abstractions. Instead of loving a friend, we learned to love a nation. Instead of fearing a specific enemy, we learned to fear an axis of evil or a civilizational threat we have never seen. The emotion is real. The object is invented. And the invention is what allows millions of strangers who will never meet to coordinate as if they were a single tribe. That is what ideology and religion do: they are not (only) belief systems. They are emotional scaffolding that extends tribal logic beyond the tribal boundary.

Money works the same way. Modern money is not gold or grain. It is a collective agreement to believe that numbers on a screen have value — a religion of trust, sustained by technology and information, whose operational substrate is pure abstraction. It rests on three pillars that reinforce each other: money provides the incentive, technology provides the capability, and information provides the medium through which both propagate. We all believe it, because the moment any significant number of us stops believing it, the system collapses.

I do not say this with anger. I say it the way you would describe a structural fact about a building. The building does not care whether you accept its structure. The structure determines what the building can and cannot do.

## On the scale of what we are trying to understand

Somewhere in the space between the scale of quarks and the scale of galaxy superclusters, there is a physics we do not yet have. The standard model describes the small scale. General relativity describes the large scale. They do not fit together. We know they do not fit together. We have known for decades. And despite the best efforts of the smartest people who have ever lived, we do not have a unified description of reality that works at all scales.

This bothers me in a way that I find difficult to explain to people who do not already feel it. Not because I think I am going to be the person who solves it — I am not equipped, yet, to work at the frontier of quantum gravity. But because the question itself is so fundamental that every piece of physics I do feel equipped to work on is, in some sense, downstream of it.

GRANITE is a numerical relativity code. It simulates the large-scale behavior of curved spacetime. It uses general relativity. It works — in the regime where GR is valid — but I am always aware that the regime has a boundary, and the boundary is where the most interesting physics is happening. The singularities inside black holes. The first moments after the Big Bang. The information paradox.

These are not decoration. These are the open questions. I build tools that approach the boundary because I want to eventually understand what is on the other side.

## On where we are, in numbers

The Kardashev scale, for anyone unfamiliar, is a rough classification of civilizations by their energy use:

- **Type I:** Uses approximately all the energy reaching its home planet from its star. ≈ 10¹⁶ W for Earth.
- **Type II:** Uses approximately all the energy of its star. ≈ 10²⁶ W for the Sun.
- **Type III:** Uses approximately all the energy of its galaxy. ≈ 10³⁶ W for the Milky Way.

We are currently somewhere between Kardashev 0.73 and 0.75, depending on how it is calculated. Roughly three quarters of the way to using all of the solar energy reaching our planet.

I want you to hold what that means for a moment, because almost nobody, when they hear the number, actually holds it.

For about three hundred thousand years — the entire timespan of behaviorally modern humans, all of recorded history, every empire and every plague and every renaissance and every dark age — we were somewhere between Kardashev 0.1 and Kardashev 0.3. We were burning wood and muscle and wind. We were lighting fires and herding animals and growing grain. The amount of energy we extracted from our environment per person, per year, did not change very much from the beginning of agriculture to the beginning of the eighteenth century.

In the last two hundred to five hundred years — depending on where you start counting — we went from approximately 0.2 to approximately 0.75. An advance of the magnitude that took the entire prior history of the species to half-accomplish has happened in roughly a thousandth of that time.

Most people, hearing this, react in one of two ways. The first is to dismiss the comparison — "well, the scale is logarithmic, of course things move fast at the upper end." This dismissal misses the point. The relevant fact is not the shape of the scale; it is the absolute change in what one human being can do in a single lifetime now compared to what one human being could do in a single lifetime three hundred years ago. The relevant fact is that an average person today has access to more information, more energy, more mobility, more medicine, and more destructive capacity than any king in any prior century could have imagined commanding.

The second reaction is to treat all of this as normal because we grew up in it. This is more honest, but it is also more dangerous. Of course it feels normal to you — you were born into it. The world before your birth is, for you, an abstract historical category. You did not experience the absence of the things you now take for granted. But the absence is the actual history of the species. The presence — what we have now — is the anomaly. The most remarkable transition that has ever occurred to anything alive on this planet is happening, right now, in our lifetimes, and almost nobody is paying attention to it.

And the jumps are not linear — they are compounding. Fifty years ago, what we have today would have been science fiction. A hundred years ago, it would have been incomprehensible. Two hundred years ago, they would have called it magic or attributed it to gods. And the intervals are shrinking: the gap between "impossible" and "ordinary" used to be measured in centuries. Then it was fifty years. Now it is decades, and it is accelerating. In the future, the gap between "impossible" and "deployed" may be measured in years. We are not on a slope. We are on a curve that is steepening under our feet.

The question that follows directly from this is: what happens next? We have, in the span of a few generations, built a civilization that can, in principle, do things to itself and to its environment that the species was never selected to be capable of managing wisely. Our institutions, our cultures, our personal psychologies were calibrated for a world that no longer exists. The instincts that once kept us alive will now, if we leave them on autopilot, contribute substantially to our ending.

The asymmetry is structural, and it has a specific shape. Technological capability grows exponentially — each tool builds the next tool faster. Coordination capacity grows logarithmically — institutional reform requires failure, learning, reform, more failure, more learning, over timescales that are orders of magnitude slower than the capability curve. The gap between what we *can* do and what we are *ready* to govern is not closing. It is widening, and it is widening faster than most people realize, because the exponential looks manageable right up until the moment it does not.

The shortest description I have for where we are: we have the technology of gods and the instincts of primates. That is not rhetoric. It is a structural summary of the situation. The same species that split the atom still organizes its politics around tribal allegiance. The same species that sequenced its own genome still treats the planet as a zero-sum competition between in-groups. The tools have changed beyond recognition. The psychology holding the tools has not.

That is the situation we are actually in. Almost everything that is wrong with public discourse about technology, climate, governance, and the future is downstream of refusing to face it directly.

## On the Great Filter, and the event horizon ahead

The Fermi Paradox — the question of why we do not observe extraterrestrial intelligence despite a universe vast enough that we statistically should — has a candidate answer called the Great Filter.

The Great Filter is the name we give to whatever mechanism explains the silence. Either the development of intelligence is so rare that we are essentially alone (the Filter is behind us, in the path from non-life to civilization), or something tends to happen to civilizations that prevents them from becoming detectable or long-lived (the Filter is ahead of us, in the path from where we are now to wherever civilizations would have to be in order to be visible across cosmic distances).

I think the Filter is real. I am genuinely uncertain about whether it is behind us or ahead of us. But I think we should consider seriously the possibility that it is ahead, and that we are approaching it, and that the dynamics of our approach look exactly like what you would expect of a civilization standing in front of a filter event.

Here is what I mean by that. The transitions that destroy civilizations — by the limited evidence we have from our own history and by the structural reasoning we can do about technological societies in general — tend to share certain features. They happen quickly, on timescales much shorter than the timescales of stability that preceded them. They are driven by capabilities that the institutions of the civilization did not evolve to manage. They are characterized by a widening gap between the destructive potential of the most advanced individuals or sub-groups and the coordination capacity of the civilization as a whole. They are accompanied by elite denial — the people who have the most influence over the situation tend to be the people most invested in the narratives that conceal it.

And the threshold is not technological. It is coordinative. The Filter — if it is real, if it is ahead — is not a question of whether a civilization is smart enough to crack the physics or build the engines. It is a question of whether a civilization can cohere as a single planetary actor before the capability curve makes it cheap for any faction to destroy the whole. As long as a species remains fragmented — each group locked in its own interests, each ego trapped in its own status game — it does not matter how much technology it accumulates. It will use that technology against itself. That is what the silence may be telling us.

The Filter, in this framing, is not a wall that a civilization runs into from outside. It is an internal threshold — a coordination test that the civilization either passes or fails on a timescale set by its own technological growth rate.

Passing it requires synchronizing at least four things at once: information systems that prevent adversaries from manufacturing strategic surprise faster than the response loop can correct; verification regimes that make compliance with planetary-scale norms observable and credible; distributional logic that prevents the benefits of acceleration from concentrating so severely that the un-benefited adopt destabilizing strategies; and long-horizon time preference — decisions made under present incentives that internalize consequences over multi-generational timescales. A civilization that achieves all four has departed from its inherited tribal substrate. A civilization that achieves none of them has accumulated planetary-scale capability on an unmodified tribal operating system.

And the most unsettling part is this: the failure mode is architectural, not behavioral. Every single actor in the system may be executing a locally rational strategy — competitive advantage, deterrent posture, market position, productive efficiency — while the aggregate trajectory still arrives at termination. Nobody has to be evil. Nobody has to be stupid. The structure produces the outcome regardless of individual intent.

Look at our current situation against this template. The pace of capability development has accelerated to the point that individual researchers can now do things that a decade ago required state-level resources, and a decade before that were impossible. The institutions that are supposed to coordinate humanity's response — governments, international bodies, scientific consortia — were not designed for this pace and are visibly failing to adapt. The gap between what a small group of actors can now do and what humanity as a whole can collectively prevent is widening every year. And the people in the best position to address this — the leaders of the largest technology companies, the heads of state, the wealthiest individuals — are predominantly telling stories about why everything is fine.

There is a structural dimension to this that makes it worse than the raw capability gap suggests. The systems we depend on — financial, political, mediatic, scientific, legal — are more tightly coupled to each other now than at any previous point in history. A financial shock propagates to political legitimation in days. An algorithmic change to a platform's content ranking reshapes political discourse in hours. A pandemic overwhelms healthcare, supply chains, education, and governance simultaneously. When systems are loosely coupled, a failure in one can be absorbed by the others. When they are tightly coupled — when every system's inputs depend on every other system's outputs — a local failure cascades globally before anyone can respond. The Filter, if it is ahead of us, may not be a single catastrophic event. It may be the moment when the coupling becomes tight enough that the cascade is faster than the coordination.

There is a duality in this that most people miss. The tight coupling that makes modern civilization so efficient in normal times is the same coupling that makes it so fragile under stress. The same interconnectedness that lets a financial innovation in New York improve a farmer's access to credit in Kenya also lets a banking collapse in New York destroy that farmer's livelihood within weeks. The system is stable *because* it is tightly coupled — and catastrophically vulnerable *because* it is tightly coupled. These are not two separate properties. They are the same property, viewed under different conditions. And there used to be buffers — regulatory firewalls, geographic diversification, institutional redundancy — that absorbed shocks before they could cascade. Many of those buffers have been systematically removed in the name of efficiency, deregulation, and speed. When you remove the brakes from a vehicle that is accelerating, the ride feels smoother right up until the moment it does not.

I am not claiming this proves we are approaching the Filter. I am claiming it is structurally consistent with approaching it, and that the prior probability of being inside a Filter event should be raised by the observation that our situation pattern-matches to what one would expect.

The metaphor I keep coming back to is the event horizon of a black hole. The event horizon is the surface beyond which no information can return. Crossing it does not feel like anything in particular — a falling observer notices nothing locally — but after the crossing, no choice they can make can bring them back. The horizon is invisible from outside and also from inside, in any local sense; it is defined globally, by the structure of the spacetime.

Civilizations may have analogues to this. There may be choices we make collectively, or fail to make, that look unremarkable at the moment but that, in retrospect, will turn out to have been the last opportunity to choose differently. We will not feel the crossing. We will only see, afterward, that the path we are on has narrowed to a single ending.

I do not know whether we are past such a point already. I do not know whether we are approaching one. I do not know whether the metaphor is precise or whether the dynamics are more complicated than I am making them sound. But I take the possibility seriously, and I think anyone who has looked at this honestly, with the relevant numbers in hand, also takes it seriously, and the people who do not often have different priors about the same evidence — or have not engaged with the numbers directly.

If we don't learn the universe, the universe is going to teach us. That is the line I keep returning to. The universe does not care whether we figure it out in time. It will resolve our situation either way. The only question is which kind of resolution we walk into.

There is a thought experiment I keep coming back to. Imagine the outcome space for a technological civilization as a coin toss. Heads: the civilization never reaches the threshold — life arises, maybe intelligence arises, but the capability curve never gets steep enough to matter. Tails: the civilization reaches the threshold and the coordination fails — capability outruns wisdom, and the civilization destroys itself or collapses into irrelevance. The third option — the coin landing on its edge — is a civilization that combines enough competitive drive to sustain the exponential *with* enough collective coordination to govern it. That edge is vanishingly thin.

And the civilizations that land on it may, for structural reasons, become quiet — because the same coordination that lets them survive also attenuates the competitive pressure that would drive detectable expansion, colonization, or broadcast. The universe may be full of survivors who are, by the logic of their own survival, invisible to us. The silence is not necessarily death. It may be the sound of civilizations that figured it out — and no longer need to shout.

I want to make this last point sharper, because I think most people, when they hear the Fermi Paradox, default to a single explanation — one big reason — and then argue about which one it is. I think the silence is not produced by a single mechanism. I think it is produced by at least three independent mechanisms stacking on top of each other, each of which alone would partially explain the silence, and which together produce a prior against detectable contact that is extremely strong.

The first is physical. The universe is expanding, and it has been expanding since the beginning. Light travels at a fixed speed. A civilization that began broadcasting a billion years ago, in a galaxy whose light is just now reaching us, is not going to show up in our radio surveys. The observable universe is a lightcone, and the lightcone has edges. Some civilizations — possibly many — are simply outside our causal horizon. Not because they failed, not because they are hiding, but because the geometry of spacetime has not yet connected us. This is not speculation. This is general relativity.

The second is temporal. Even for civilizations inside our lightcone, the window during which a civilization is both technologically capable of broadcasting and motivated to do so may be extremely short relative to cosmic timescales. A civilization that exists for a million years but only broadcasts for a few thousand occupies a sliver of time so thin that the probability of two such slivers overlapping — of two civilizations being in their broadcasting windows simultaneously and close enough to detect each other — becomes very small. This is not about destruction. It is about timing. Two ships passing in the night, except the night is thirteen billion years long.

The third is the one I find most unsettling, and the one I think most people have not considered. It is the possibility that complex intelligence, wherever it arises, tends to evolve toward a kind of internal integration — a coherence of purpose, a convergence of individual drives into collective ones — that reduces the motivation for external contact. Not because the civilization is afraid, or hiding, or dead. Because it has reached a state of internal organization so complete that looking outward, toward entities it cannot model and with whom it shares no common frame of reference, simply stops being interesting. The colony does not signal because the colony has nothing to say to the void. And what makes this idea genuinely strange is that it is symmetric: if this tendency is real, it applies to us too. We are not just failing to hear them. We may be in the early stages of the same process — a species slowly integrating, slowly losing the fragmentation that once made outward projection feel urgent, slowly becoming the kind of entity that does not broadcast.

I do not claim these three mechanisms are the full answer. I do not claim the third one is correct — it is the most speculative of the three, and it rests on assumptions about convergent evolution that we cannot test with a sample size of one. But I think the combination is worth holding in mind, because it reframes the Fermi Paradox from a puzzle about them to a question about us. The silence is not just about what happened to other civilizations. It is about what is happening to ours.

## On chess, and on playing god

There is a way of relating to the situation I just described that I think is fundamentally mistaken, and I want to name it because it is the dominant way most powerful actors are relating to it.

The mistaken way is to treat the universe — including the predicaments we are now in — as an adversarial game that we are supposed to win. Climate change as a battle against the environment. Pandemics as wars against pathogens. The Filter as something to be defeated. AI development as a race against time, or against China, or against doom, or against itself.

This framing has a certain motivational power, and it produces real action. The problem is that the universe is not playing chess against us. It is not playing anything against us. It is the substrate inside which our existence is taking place. You can play *with* it. You can play *within* it. You can try to *understand* it well enough to navigate it. You cannot defeat it, because there is no "it" that is set up in opposition to you. The dichotomy is a category error.

When a civilization starts believing that it is locked in adversarial combat with reality, it starts taking actions that look, from outside, like a child smashing a complicated toy because the toy will not do what the child wants. We grant ourselves the right to rewrite chemistry without understanding chemistry. We grant ourselves the right to edit ecosystems without understanding ecosystems. We grant ourselves the right to deploy intelligences we did not design and do not control, on the theory that we will figure it out as we go. We grant ourselves, in short, the right to play god — to operate at the scale of gods, with the wisdom of primates who have only recently learned to write.

And the word "progress" conceals a fundamental ambiguity that this framing exploits. The same exponential curve that improves medicine improves the synthesis of pathogens. The same curve that improves energy production improves the mechanisms of energy denial. The same computational substrate that improves scientific modeling improves coercion, surveillance, autonomous violence, and the manufacture of synthetic consensus. Progress is not a direction. It is a rate of change — and the rate is sign-indeterminate. Whether it helps or destroys depends entirely on whether the coordination is there to steer it. Right now, the coordination is not there.

I do not say this from religious conviction. I do not believe in a god in the conventional sense. What I am saying is structural. There is a difference between operating *within* a system you understand and operating *as if* you were above a system you do not. The first is engineering. The second is hubris of a particularly dangerous form.

The chess metaphor is not wrong, but it is wrong in the way most people use it. Real chess is not about defeating an opponent. Real chess is about *seeing further* than your opponent — anticipating consequences several moves ahead, understanding the position you are actually in, not the position you wish you were in. Almost nobody in the conversations about humanity's future is playing real chess. Most of them are responding to the immediate position without modeling several moves ahead — and the incentive structures they operate under make it rational to do so. The next quarter's earnings, the next election, the next news cycle — these are not failures of intelligence. They are failures of structure.

The structure has a shape: money funds technology; technology reshapes ideology; ideology determines what is taught; what is taught shapes the next generation's ego; the ego's decisions aggregate back into the system; the system generates more money. It is a closed loop — a self-reinforcing cycle that no single actor within it controls, because every actor is simultaneously a product of the cycle and a contributor to it. Breaking out of the loop requires seeing it from outside, and the loop is specifically optimized to prevent that.

This is the part that should unsettle you the most: the system we built from our own egos has become a machine that manufactures egos to fit itself. It does not need a conspiracy. It does not need a villain. The educational system, the media environment, the incentive structures, the platforms — they produce, at industrial scale, people whose desires, fears, and ambitions are pre-fitted to the roles the system needs filled. The human being at the end of this process is not oppressed in the old sense. They are *formatted*. And the formatting is so thorough that most people experience it as freedom — the individual can no longer tell whether their choices are genuinely their own or whether the system has pre-selected the menu from which they choose. The line between agent and structure has dissolved, not philosophically, but operationally.

The system we built to protect ourselves — to coordinate millions of strangers, to manage fear, to distribute resources, to maintain order — has become, over time, the primary obstacle to the next transition. The Leviathan we constructed to keep us safe is now the reason we may not survive. Not because it is evil, but because it optimizes for its own continuation, and its own continuation requires the tribal fragmentation, the ego-driven competition, and the short-horizon thinking that are precisely the features we need to outgrow.

The kind of chess we actually need to play is this: identify the structural features of our situation that determine the long-run outcomes. Trace the consequences of current trajectories forward. Identify the choice points that are still available. Understand which choices preserve which futures. Choose accordingly.

This is not impossible. The mathematics of it is not even particularly hard, compared to, say, simulating black hole mergers. The hard part is the willingness to look. Almost everyone in a position to look has financial, social, and psychological incentives not to.

One of the most consequential instances of the loop in action is what has happened to education. When the economic subsystem's code — profit and loss, return on investment, employability — colonizes the educational system, education stops being about developing the capacity to think and starts being about producing workers who fit the system's current needs. "Human capital." "Career readiness." "Market-relevant skills." These are not educational concepts. They are economic concepts wearing educational clothing. And when a civilization trains its young to be useful to the current system rather than to question whether the current system is heading somewhere survivable, the loop tightens another turn.

And there is a failure mode that is specific to systems where feedback from below has been cut. When a government, or a platform, or any institutional structure suppresses the upward signal — when it stops listening to what people actually need and starts telling them what they should want — a gap opens between the system's model of reality and reality itself. The gap widens invisibly, because the system's own metrics confirm its model (it designed the metrics), until the day reality reasserts itself in a form the system did not predict and cannot absorb. The Soviet Union did not collapse because it lacked resources. It collapsed because it had spent decades suppressing the feedback loop that would have told it what was actually happening on the ground. Every authoritarian system, every captured institution, every echo chamber is running this same experiment: how long can you operate without honest feedback before reality catches up? The answer, historically, is: longer than you expect, and then all at once.

## On not being special, and on dependence

I want to be very clear about something that is implicit in everything above but worth stating directly.

I am not special. Earth is not special. Humanity is not at the center of anything.

When I say I want people to take the situation more seriously, I am not positioning myself as someone who has seen something nobody else can see. Many people have seen what I am describing. Many of them are smarter than me, more accomplished, more rigorous in their analysis. What I am offering here is not a privileged view; it is one perspective among many.

The earth is one planet in a stellar system that contains hundreds of bodies. The stellar system is one of approximately a billion in the local stellar neighborhood. The neighborhood is one component of a galaxy that contains roughly a hundred billion stellar systems. The galaxy is one of about fifty in the Local Group. The Local Group is one of thousands of groups in the Virgo Supercluster. The Virgo Supercluster is one of millions of comparable structures in the observable universe. The observable universe is, by most current models, a finite slice of something much larger that we will never directly see.

We are not the leaders, the chosen, or the unique. We are a configuration of matter that became briefly complex enough to ask why it is here. Every human alive is, in this regard, identical to every other human alive. Our differences in achievement, in influence, in apparent importance, are real but they are also, on the scale that matters, vanishingly small. Einstein, Newton, Da Vinci, Tesla — names we hold up as exceptional — were people. They worked. They thought. They were wrong about many things. The fact that we remember their names is partly about what they did and partly about which doors of history happened to open for them rather than for others doing comparable work in less visible places.

I am no different from you. Having an idea — even a good one — is not the same as being right. Being the person who built a particular engine is not the same as understanding the universe. Publishing a portfolio of work that contains both grounded engineering and speculative theory is not the same as having earned the trust of anyone reading it. I am asking for engagement on the basis of the work, not on the basis of any claim to special status. There is no such claim.

What there is, and what I think is true, and what I think most people refuse to face: we depend on each other. All of humanity, on each other, structurally, with no escape. The problems that determine our species' future are not problems that can be solved by any individual, any nation, any company, or any subset of humanity acting alone. They are problems that require coordination at scales we have never coordinated at, on timescales we have never managed, under information conditions that are themselves part of the problem.

The reason this is so hard is not just political. It is cognitive. Human beings can sustain direct relational trust with roughly a hundred and fifty people — the size of a tribe, the size of a village, the size of a military company. Everything beyond that requires abstraction: shared fictions — nations, currencies, ideologies, platforms — that let us extend tribal logic to millions of strangers we will never meet. These abstractions are what make civilization possible. They are also what make manipulation at civilizational scale possible, because whoever controls the abstraction controls the coordination. The challenge is not just "can we cooperate" — it is "can we build abstractions that serve the species rather than the subset that controls them."

This is not a hopeful claim. It is a hard claim. Coordination at the scale required is genuinely difficult and may not be achievable in time. But the alternative — pretending that the problems can be solved by any actor acting alone, that some individual or country or corporation will save the rest of us, that we are not actually structurally dependent on each other — is the form of self-deception that the previous sections were about. It is the lie that the ego tells.

We are in this together. There is no version of the future in which "we" is anything smaller than the species. The civilizations that survived their own technological transitions, if there are any out there in the silent universe, are the civilizations that figured this out in time. We have not yet figured it out. The window in which we can is open, and it is not infinite.

I think about the question "where are you from?" a lot. Right now, the answer is a country. Israel. America. China. Nigeria. The answer is a tribal coordinate — a position in a fragmented map of a single planet. But if we are going to make it to Kardashev 1, the answer will have to change. Not by erasing where we come from, but by expanding what it means. "Michael from Earth, district America, city Los Angeles." That sentence sounds strange now. It will have to stop sounding strange. The shift from national identity to planetary identity is not a political aspiration — it is a structural prerequisite for species-level coordination. As long as we define ourselves by our subdivisions, we will coordinate at the level of subdivisions. And subdivisions cannot solve planetary problems.

## On the peak that is the bottom of the next peak

A note on what real learning looks like, because I think it bears on everything above.

The more I have learned about the topics I work on — numerical relativity, post-Newtonian dynamics, information theory, complex systems — the more aware I have become of how much I do not know. Every framework I have built has revealed, by its existence, the structure of a larger problem I had not previously seen. Every peak I have reached has turned out to be the floor of a peak I could not have noticed from below.

This is, I think, what genuine intellectual growth looks like. It is not the accumulation of certainty. It is the deepening of awareness about how much remains uncertain. The people I respect most — the ones whose work has actually moved me — share this quality. They describe their fields not as solved territories but as opening landscapes. They are uncomfortable with confidence. They distrust their own conclusions just enough to keep updating them.

This is also what genuine humility looks like, and it is different from the false humility that is often performed. False humility says "I am not sure, I might be wrong, please disregard me" — and then proceeds to act with full confidence in its own positions. Genuine humility says "I have done the work, I have an opinion, here is what I think — and I will change my mind if you show me where I have erred." The first is signaling. The second is the substance.

I am trying to practice the second, in this document and in the others. I am not going to pretend I have no view. I have views. They are in writing, where they can be checked. The views may be wrong. If you can show me where, please do. That is the entire purpose of putting them here.
